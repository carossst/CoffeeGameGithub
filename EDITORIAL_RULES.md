# Editorial Rules

These rules apply to quiz content, especially rule-based content and annual rule-change items.

## Source Priority

1. Official source material for the topic
2. Primary reference documentation
3. Secondary sources such as articles, videos, or commentary

Secondary sources may help with framing or prioritization, but they do not override the primary source.

## Core Principle

Content may be rewritten for readability, but not at the cost of precision.

Do not remove:
- a condition
- a time boundary
- a positional boundary
- a scope qualifier
- an enforcement qualifier
- a terminology distinction that matters in the primary source

If the source includes a limiting detail, keep it.

## Rewrite Standard

Allowed:
- shorter sentence structure
- cleaner word order
- less awkward phrasing
- removal of redundancy that does not change meaning
- more everyday wording when precision is fully preserved

Not allowed:
- replacing a precise rule with a looser summary
- removing a limiting clause to make the sentence shorter
- using commentary language in place of source language
- borrowing a simplification from a secondary source if the primary source is more precise
- replacing precise wording with more casual wording if a condition, limit, or exception is lost

## Working Preference

Fast reading is good.

But precise content wins over punch.

The target is:
- quick to read
- easy to parse
- still faithful to the source
- natural enough to sound like clear player-facing language

## Explanation Tone

Explanations should sound clearer and more player-facing than the source.

Target tone:
- direct
- plainspoken
- conversational without slang
- less bureaucratic
- still exact on conditions, limits, and outcomes

Allowed tone shifts in explanations:
- `There is no` -> `There's no`
- `does not` -> `doesn't`
- `is not` -> `isn't`
- `are not` -> `aren't`
- `X is a fault.` -> `If X happens, it's a fault.` when that reads more naturally
- `may` -> `can` when this does not weaken or broaden the source meaning

Preferred explanation style:
- say what happens on court or in play
- favor the practical consequence over abstract category labels
- use everyday player language when precision is preserved
- keep the first line as the fastest useful takeaway

Not allowed:
- adding personality, jokes, or hype
- replacing a defined term with a vague synonym
- simplifying away timing, scope, or enforcement conditions
- turning a precise effect into commentary or interpretation
- making the wording friendlier if that changes who can act, when, or with what consequence

## False-Item Standard

For items whose correct answer is `false`:
- prefer a false affirmative statement
- avoid building the item around `not`, `never`, `may not`, `must not`, or similar negative phrasing when a cleaner false affirmative is available
- avoid double-negation logic
- avoid wording traps that test sentence parsing more than topic knowledge

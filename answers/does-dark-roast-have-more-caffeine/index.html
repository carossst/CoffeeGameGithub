Does dark roast coffee have more caffeine than light roast?

No, not by default. In a 2024 lab study of filter brews, dark roasts generally had lower caffeine concentrations than light and medium roasts when everything else was held the same.

Last checked: 2026-11-17

What the study measured

Researchers at Berry College and Drexel University tested 30 combinations of green coffee variety, degree of roast, and brew time. They brewed each sample with an AeroPress at a 15:1 mass ratio of water to ground coffee.

They measured extraction yield with refractometry and caffeine content with HPLC, a standard laboratory method. They also used scanning electron microscopy to measure the porosity of the roasted beans.

What it found

Under identical brewing conditions, caffeine concentrations in the brews were generally lower for dark roasts than for light and medium roasts.

However, when the comparison was made at identical extraction yields, dark roasts generally showed higher caffeine concentrations than lighter roasts. The authors suggest that two effects compete: soluble compounds are lost or broken down during roasting, while roasting also makes the bean more porous.

Extraction yields generally dropped for roast batches that lost more than about 12 to 14 percent of their mass in the roaster.

Why the question is tricky

The statement mixes up two different things: how much caffeine is in the bean and how much ends up in the cup. Roast level changes how easily the bean gives up what it holds, so there is no simple rule that darker always means more caffeine.

The study measured caffeine directly in the brew. It did not measure how strong the coffee tastes, which is a separate question.

What this page does not claim

This is one study of AeroPress-style filter brews at one ratio. Other brewing methods and recipes can behave differently, and the study does not give a single number that applies to every cup.

Common questions

Does dark roast coffee have more caffeine than light roast?

Not by default. In a 2024 study of filter brews under identical brewing conditions, dark roasts generally had lower caffeine concentrations than light and medium roasts.

Why can the same study find both results?

Because it compared two things. Under identical brewing conditions dark roasts were generally lower, but at identical extraction yields dark roasts were generally higher. Roast level changes how much caffeine reaches the cup.

Does roasting destroy caffeine?

This page does not claim that. The study measured caffeine in the brew, not in the bean, so it cannot say how much caffeine the roast itself removes.
